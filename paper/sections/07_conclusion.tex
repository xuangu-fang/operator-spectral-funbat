\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The form of a governing equation -- not its coefficients, not its solution -- is
enough to fix the per-mode kernels of a functional tensor decomposition.  The
construction is three steps, leaves no kernel parameters to learn, and drops into
an existing model in place of its generic kernels, so it composes with better
tensor models rather than competing with them.

What we did not find is a margin over a well-tuned generic kernel.  Given a
length scale chosen against the held-out region, a plain Mat\'ern matches our
derived spectra everywhere we measured, and the first version of this work
claimed otherwise only because its search grid stopped short of the baseline's
own optimum.

What we did find is that in the setting this work is about, that length scale
cannot be chosen.  When sensors are confined to a wall or a patch, every point a
practitioner can hold out lies inside the same region, so validation scores
interpolation while deployment requires extrapolation, and it selects a kernel
three to five times too short.  Following that choice costs $0.27$ NRMSE at a
single instrumented wall, against $0.0003$ when sensors are scattered.  A kernel
read off the equation needs no such split and lands where the oracle would have.

The two halves are one statement rather than a result and an excuse: \emph{where
you can validate, you do not need physics; where you need physics, you cannot
validate}.  It also says where not to use this.  Scattered sensors do not need
it.  Geometries demanding extrapolation along two axes at once are not merely
beyond it -- there it is worse than predicting the mean.

The construction is a prior over how a field varies along each axis, obtained
without fitting.  Whether such a representation transfers to tasks other than
reconstruction -- generation, or fields it was not fitted to -- is the question
we would ask next, and this paper does not answer it.
