\section{When this does not work}
\label{sec:limitations}

Three separate conditions have to hold, and each of them rejected something we
tried.  We state them as screens because that is how we used them: measured
before any method was run, on fields we then did not use.

\paragraph{The operator must suppress much of the spectrum.}
This is not an extra assumption layered on top of low-rank tensor completion --
it is the same condition seen from the other side.  An operator that suppresses
little leaves a broad spectrum, a field with a broad spectrum is not low rank,
and the host model fails before any kernel is chosen.  We fit a fully observed
low-rank model at the ranks we intend to use and require its relative error to be
comfortably below $1$.

\emph{Rejected: broad-spectrum turbulence.}  Kolmogorov flow at
$\mathrm{Re} = 40$ needs $63\%$ of the modes along each spatial axis to carry
$95\%$ of the energy, and a fully observed rank-$40$ CP still leaves $0.298$
relative error.  \emph{Rejected: pattern-forming reaction-diffusion.}  PDEBench
2D diffusion-reaction gives the same $63\%$, unchanged by resolution and by
whether one strides or block-averages, so it is intrinsic rather than an
aliasing artefact; held-out NRMSE stayed above $1$ for every combination of host
model, basis, feature budget and observation ratio we tried.

\paragraph{The spectrum must be approximately separable across axes.}
It is tempting to expect the construction to shine where a generic monotone
kernel cannot follow the spectrum at all.  The opposite happens.  A linear
Swift--Hohenberg operator has its response minimised on a ring $\lvert k \rvert
= k_0$, and a ring is maximally non-separable: an axis-separable approximation
fills the square instead of the ring.  At $1\%$ observed the band-pass operator
prior scores $0.92$ against $0.74$ for a generic dictionary.  Separability, not
expressiveness, is what the construction requires.

\paragraph{The sensors must be able to see the region of interest.}
This screen is specific to the confined-layout setting and it rejected two
designs of our own.  A field driven by independent noise everywhere is not
determined by its boundary at all -- the interior carries forcing no boundary
sensor observes -- and every method landed near $0.9$ there, as information alone
predicts.  A single narrow plume fails for the opposite reason: its signal
reaches the wall attenuated to nothing.  We measure the boundary's share of the
field's energy divided by its share of the cells, and require at least $0.5$.
The rejected plume scores $0.160$ with wall SNR $7.6$; the fields we use score
$0.986$ and $1.035$ with wall SNR $19$.  An earlier version of this screen
demanded a ratio of at least $1$, which would have rejected our own main field at
$0.986$ -- a threshold sitting on the accepted set is not measuring anything, and
we recalibrated it against the design it was introduced to reject.

\paragraph{Extrapolating along two axes at once is worse than useless.}
The corner-patch row of \Cref{tab:layouts} is a loss, and a large one: we score
$1.0212$, worse than predicting the mean and worse than every baseline including
the deployable ones.  The prior does not degrade gracefully into a weak
smoothness assumption when the geometry defeats it; it actively hurts.  The
layouts where it helps are those forcing extrapolation along a single axis, which
is what a per-mode spectrum is a statement about.

\paragraph{Other limitations.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{We do not beat a well-tuned kernel; we match it.}  The claim in
        this paper is that the tuning is unavailable, not that the tuned answer
        is beatable.  Wherever a practitioner \emph{can} validate -- scattered
        sensors, or any layout where held-out data resembles the target region --
        this construction has nothing to offer, and \Cref{tab:layouts} shows it
        losing slightly there.
  \item The evidence is on fields from a linear forced PDE.  Nonlinear and
        variable-coefficient operators are only approximated by a linearised
        constant-coefficient form, and we do not measure how far that degrades.
  \item \textbf{The posterior predictive is under-covered in the sparse regime.}
        At $1\%$ observed a nominal $95\%$ interval covers $83.7\%$ with our
        prior and $77.5\%$ with the generic one.  Ours is the better calibrated
        of the two and its advantage again concentrates where data is scarce, but
        it does not fix the under-coverage, which belongs to the host model.  Our
        intervals are also wider ($1.14$ versus $1.00$), so part of that
        advantage is honesty about uncertainty rather than accuracy.  We claim a
        better prior for point prediction, not better uncertainty quantification.
  \item Uncertainty is a mean-field variational posterior over a finite feature
        basis, not an exact GP posterior, and the NLL carries $64$-sample
        Monte-Carlo error.
  \item The domain is a regular grid with axis-aligned sensor regions.  Irregular
        domains, curved boundaries and moving sensors are untested.
\end{enumerate}
