\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{The setting: sensors you cannot place freely}
A room contains several gas leaks.  The field obeys
$\partial_t u = D_x \partial_{xx} u + D_y \partial_{yy} u - r u$ with zero flux at
the walls, and away from the leaks it is source-free, so the interior is
genuinely constrained by what happens at the boundary.  We record it as a
$[t,x,y]$ tensor at $64 \times 64$ in space and $64$ steps in time.

What we vary is the one thing a practitioner does not get to choose: \emph{where
the sensors are}.  Instruments attach to walls, to a strip along one wall, to a
band set slightly in from the wall, or to a single instrumented patch.  Every
layout observes the same $1\%$ of entries; only their arrangement differs.  This
turns reconstruction from interpolation between neighbours into extrapolation
into a region no sensor ever sees, and that is the regime in which a smoothness
prior has nothing left to say.

\paragraph{What the method is told.}  The equation's \emph{form} and nominal
coefficients that are deliberately not the generating ones: the field is run at
$D = (0.02, 0.006)$, $r = 0.04$ and the prior is built from $D = (0.03, 0.012)$,
$r = 0.06$.  No coefficient is fitted and no tuning data is used.

\paragraph{Everything else is held fixed.}  One field family, one rank, one step
budget, one optimiser, one set of baselines.  The table is layout $\times$
method, and nothing else moves between its cells.

\subsection{Main result: with sensors confined, the kernel cannot be chosen from data}

\input{sections/table_layouts}

The table has three tiers and they differ in one thing only: what data chose the
kernel.  The \emph{oracle} Mat\'ern minimises the true held-out error over a
length-scale grid; no practitioner can run it, and it is marked $\star$
throughout.  The \emph{deployable} Mat\'ern does what one actually can -- hold
out a quarter of the sensor readings and score candidates on those.  Our arm
uses no tuning data at all: the spectra come from the equation's form with
coefficients wrong by $50\%$.

\paragraph{We do not beat a tuned kernel.  We tie it.}
At one wall the oracle reaches $0.5449$ and we reach $0.5448$; under random masks
every arm lands within $0.002$ of every other.  A margin over a properly tuned
generic kernel is not what this construction buys, and an earlier version of this
table claimed one only because its length-scale grid stopped short of the
baseline's own optimum (\Cref{app:fairness}).

\paragraph{What it buys is not having to tune, in a setting where tuning fails.}
The last column is the cost of choosing the length scale from data a practitioner
can collect.  Under random masks that cost is $+0.0003$, which is nothing:
held-out sensor readings sit among the observed ones, so validation scores the
same thing deployment asks for, and anyone can find the right kernel.  Confine
the sensors and the cost grows with the confinement, reaching $+0.176$ at a
single wall -- an error $32\%$ worse than the oracle, from following the choice
validation actually makes.

\paragraph{The mechanism is visible in the hyper-parameters, not only in the
error.}  At one wall, validation picks length scales $0.5, 0.5, 0.8$ across
seeds while the test set wants $1.6, 2.4, 2.4$.  It is not noisy; it is
consistently three to five times too short, and the reason is structural.  Every
point that can be held out lies inside the same strip, so validation measures
interpolation within the strip, while deployment requires extrapolation across
the room.  Those two tasks want different kernels, and no amount of held-out
sensor data reveals the difference.  Under random masks the same procedure picks
correctly, which is the control: the split fails only when it stops resembling
the task.

\paragraph{Reading the two facts together.}
The setting that makes physics unnecessary and the setting that makes tuning
impossible are the same axis, running in opposite directions.  Where sensors are
scattered, a generic kernel is adequate and can be tuned, so there is nothing to
contribute.  Where sensors are confined, a generic kernel would be adequate
\emph{if} it could be tuned, and it cannot.  The equation supplies the same
answer the oracle would have found, without needing the data that would reveal
it.

\paragraph{Coverage does not order the rows.}  The four-wall ring reaches $12\%$
of the room and the corner patch $10\%$, and the ring does far better; the corner
demands extrapolation along two axes at once and every method lands near
$0.9$, close enough to predicting the mean that no comparison there means much.
What matters is the shape of the unobserved region, not its size.

\subsection{The control: when sensors can go anywhere, physics is not needed}
\label{sec:random-control}

Under uniformly random masks all three arms tie at $0.053$
(\Cref{tab:layouts}).  That is the expected result and we report it as a
control rather than as a disappointment: reconstruction from scattered samples
is interpolation between neighbours, and a smoothness prior is already a
sufficient description of that.  A prior that claimed an advantage here would be
claiming to know something the data already determines.

The same reading applies within the random-mask setting as the sample size
varies.  Below we re-use an earlier study on a stochastically forced field at
$32^3$ with uniformly random masks, where the margin over a parameter-matched
generic dictionary falls from $0.040$ at $1\%$ observed to $0.003$ at $5\%$.

\paragraph{Pooled over fifteen seeds at $1\%$.}
PDE-form kernels reach $0.4217 \pm 0.0470$ against $0.4464 \pm 0.0545$ for the
parameter-matched generic dictionary and $0.5699$ for nearest neighbour, with a
fully observed Tucker ceiling of $0.1791$.  The paired margin is
$0.0247 \pm 0.0235$, a $5.5\%$ relative improvement on 13 of 15 seeds; the sign
test gives $p = 0.004$ and a Wilcoxon signed-rank test $p = 0.0006$.  The test is
paired because all arms share the field, mask and noise within a seed.

\paragraph{Both arms are run at their own best setting.}
An earlier version of this table used per-mode/rank routing for both arms and
reported $15/15$ with an $8.0\%$ margin.  The ablation shows that routing
over-fits at $1\%$ observed and costs the generic dictionary $0.042$ against
$0.023$ for the operator bank, so that setting handicaps the baseline more than
it handicaps us and overstates the effect by roughly $1.6\times$.  We therefore
give each arm the routing that is best for it, which is global for both.

Discrete low-rank completion (CP and Tucker with EM imputation) does not beat

advantage no deployable method has.

  \centering

    anisotropic diffusion  & 0.4383 & 0.4584 & $+0.0201$ & 4/5 \\
    \textbf{isotropic diffusion} & 0.4541 & 0.4595 & $\mathbf{+0.0054}$ & 3/5 \\
    advection--diffusion   & 0.4132 & 0.4361 & $+0.0229$ & 5/5 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Making the true spectrum isotropic collapses the margin from $0.020$ to $0.005$
and the wins to 3/5.  This is the sharpest evidence for the mechanism rather than
for the numbers: the generic dictionary shares its atoms across modes, so it is
only handicapped when the axes genuinely need \emph{different} spectra.  It
agrees with the oracle result, where all of the oracle's advantage also came from
tuning per mode.  Two independent controls point at the same cause.

Advection--diffusion wins on all five seeds, which needs a word because our
signed-spectrum audit lists tilted transport as a representational limit.  There
is no contradiction: that limit concerns cross-sign coupling in the \emph{full
signed} spectrum, whereas a stochastically forced advection--diffusion field at
statistical steady state still has an anisotropic low-pass \emph{even} magnitude
spectrum, which is what the construction uses.  The boundary is the signed phase
structure, not the name of the operator family.

Three readings matter.  Wrong operator families are clearly worse than the right
one ($0.472$ and $0.478$ against $0.438$), so the identity of the operator is
doing work rather than mere smoothness.  Coefficient error is tolerated
asymmetrically: scaling the diffusivities up is nearly free ($\times 3$ and
$\times 10$ stay near the nominal value) while scaling them down is not
($\times 0.3$ and $\times 0.1$ degrade markedly), which matches the physics --
under-estimating diffusion makes the prior too rough, over-estimating it merely
over-smooths.  And doubling the generic atom count makes things worse, so the
dictionary's disadvantage is not a lack of capacity.

The arms vary only the spectral bank or the routing: the exact operator spectrum
without any low-rank projection, the right form with coefficients scaled between
$0.1\times$ and $10\times$, an isotropic version of the right form, wrong operator
families, generic banks at matched and doubled size, and global versus
per-mode/rank routing.

Two arms fix the method's design.  Under the routing the method actually uses,
the nonnegative separation into four atoms scores $0.4383$ against $0.4631$ for
simply marginalising the joint spectrum, so the separation is worth keeping.  And
per-mode/rank routing scores $0.4613$, over-fitting by $0.023$ relative to the
global mixture, so the mixture is deliberately global.

Both numbers must be read at each arm's own routing.  Comparing the separation
against the marginal spectrum \emph{both} under per-mode/rank routing shows them
tied ($0.4613$ versus $0.4631$), because that setting degrades both, which would
have led us to discard the separation.

\subsection{A derived kernel versus a tuned one}
The sharpest form of the question is whether reading the kernel off the equation
gets you where tuning a kernel on the answer would.  We give a squared-exponential
kernel a length scale chosen \emph{per mode by held-out error} -- an oracle no
deployable method has, costing 21 training runs against the test set -- and
compare at $1\%$ observed with every arm at its own best routing.

  \centering

  deployable: their hyper-parameters are selected against the test set.}
  \label{tab:oracle}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{lc}
    \toprule
    & held-out NRMSE \\
    \midrule
    SE kernel, oracle length scale per mode$^\star$ & \textbf{0.4218 $\pm$ 0.0354} \\

    generic dictionary, global mixture              & 0.4584 $\pm$ 0.0326 \\
    PDE-form, per-mode/rank routing                 & 0.4613 $\pm$ 0.0282 \\
    PDE-form, marginal spectrum only                & 0.4631 $\pm$ 0.0250 \\
    SE kernel, one oracle length scale$^\star$      & 0.4646 $\pm$ 0.0190 \\
    generic dictionary, per-mode/rank routing       & 0.5008 $\pm$ 0.0215 \\

The oracle wins, on all five seeds, by $3.9\%$.  We read that as the useful
result rather than a defeat: reading the kernel off the equation, using no tuning
data at all, lands within $3.9\%$ of tuning it on the answer, while beating every
deployable alternative.

One detail confirms the mechanism.  A \emph{single} oracle-tuned length scale
shared across modes scores $0.4646$, worse than our derived kernels.  All of the
oracle's advantage therefore comes from tuning \emph{per mode} -- which is
exactly the claim, that different axes need different spectra.  The oracle finds
them by looking at the answer; we get them from the equation for free.
