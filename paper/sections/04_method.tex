\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

Given the \emph{form} of the governing equation, the kernel for every mode of the
tensor decomposition follows up to four mixture weights.  \Cref{sec:experiments}
reports the ablation fixing both design choices: the nonnegative separation is
worth $0.025$ over using the marginal spectrum directly, and the mixture must be
\emph{global} rather than per-mode/rank, which over-fits by $0.023$ at $1\%$
observed.

\subsection{From a PDE form to per-mode kernels}

\paragraph{Step 1: the operator gives the joint spectrum.}
A stationary operator driven by noise, $\Lop u = w$, has a stationary Gaussian
solution with spectral density
\begin{equation}
  \Sop(\wvec) = \bigl|\Lhat_\theta(\wvec)\bigr|^{-2}\, \Sw(\wvec).
  \label{eq:joint-spectrum}
\end{equation}
Only the functional form of $\Lhat$ is used; $\theta$ is set to nominal values.

\paragraph{Step 2: separate into per-mode atoms.}
$\Sop$ couples the axes, whereas a functional tensor needs one function per mode,
so we project it onto $Q$ nonnegative separable components,
\begin{equation}
  \min_{\lambda_q \ge 0,\; s_{dq} \ge 0}
  \Bigl\| \Sop - \sum_{q=1}^{Q} \lambda_q\, s_{1q} \circ \cdots \circ s_{Dq} \Bigr\|_F^2 ,
  \label{eq:nncp}
\end{equation}
and learn a single global mixture $\route \in \Delta^{Q-1}$ over them, shared by
every mode and rank, so that mode $d$ uses $s_d = \sum_q \route_q s_{dq}$.  Simply
marginalising $\Sop$ per axis is the cheaper alternative and is $0.025$ worse.

\paragraph{Step 3: the spectrum is the kernel.}
With $\psi_k$ the operator's eigenfunctions under the relevant boundary condition,
\begin{equation}
  k_d(x,x') = \sum_k s_d(k)\, \psi_k(x)\, \psi_k(x')
  \label{eq:psd}
\end{equation}
is positive semi-definite for any nonnegative $s_d$, being a nonnegatively
weighted sum of outer products.

\paragraph{The eigenbasis follows the boundary condition.}
This is where the construction meets reality rather than convention.  Periodic
domains give complex exponentials; no-flux boundaries give
$\psi_k(x) = \sqrt{2}\cos(\pi k x)$ with eigenvalues $(\pi k)^2$.  Initial-value
data is not periodic along time, and imposing $f(0) = f(1)$ there is a large
unphysical constraint.  On the one-wall layout, holding everything else fixed
and changing only the basis, the periodic construction scores $0.5781$ against
$0.5448$ for the no-flux cosines (three seeds, $1\%$ observed).  In the cosine
basis the pointwise variance is not constant, which is correct: on a bounded
domain the covariance is genuinely non-stationary near the edges.

\subsection{Host model}
The kernels are used by the factors of a functional Tucker decomposition,
\begin{equation}
  u(x_1,x_2,x_3) = \sum_{r_1,r_2,r_3} G_{r_1 r_2 r_3}\,
  f_{1 r_1}(x_1) f_{2 r_2}(x_2) f_{3 r_3}(x_3),
  \qquad
  f_{d r}(x) = \phi_{s_d}(x)^\top a_{dr},
  \label{eq:tucker}
\end{equation}
with per-mode ranks chosen independently and $\phi_{s_d}$ the finite feature map
of \Cref{eq:psd}.  A CP host is not sufficient: real two-dimensional fields are
fields of blobs, not outer products, and are not low CP-rank at any rank sparse
observations could identify, even though their multilinear rank is small.  Ranks
must differ per mode for the same practical reason -- a field of multilinear rank
$[2,11,11]$ needs a $242$-entry core where an equal rank of $11$ would need
$1331$.

Coefficients carry a mean-field Gaussian posterior against an $\Norm(0,I)$ prior;
the core and noise are point estimates; training maximises the usual ELBO.  None
of this is specific to our kernels, which is the point: the same host runs every
arm in \Cref{sec:experiments}, so the only variable is where the spectra come from.
